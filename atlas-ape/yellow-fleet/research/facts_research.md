# Yellow Fleet: fact check for the ~50 s Short (all ages)

Researched 20.10.2026. The confidence ratings mean:
- **high**: primary or near-primary sources agree, or there are three or more independent sources.
- **med**: there are two sources, or one first-hand source.
- **low**: there is one secondary source, or sources contradict each other.

## A. War and blockade

### A1. War start and convoy direction
- **Safe wording:** "June 5th, 1967. War breaks out, and a convoy of cargo ships is sailing north through the Suez Canal."
- **Details:** The Six-Day War began on 5 June 1967. The convoy entered from Suez Bay (Port Tewfik) early that morning, heading **NORTH** for Port Said and the Mediterranean. These were cargo liners coming home from Australia and the Far East. The convoy was ordered to anchor in the Great Bitter Lake.
  - The tankers at the front of the convoy were told to carry on to Port Said, and they escaped.
  - Two Soviet freighters at the back turned round and went back to Suez (HILL).
  - Egypt formally closed the canal on 6 June 1967.
- **⚠ This contradicts the brief:** the convoy was **northbound, not southbound**. AmusingPlanet is the only source that says "southward". The New York Times 1974 headline slug says "headed south", but I could not read the article.
- **Sources:** HILL p.3; HAPAG-1975 (the southbound convoy got through, the northbound one was stopped); SCHARRNBECK; MARE (left Suez at 6:30, northbound); WP-EN; WP-DE; WP-SV; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_of_the_Suez_Canal_(1967%E2%80%931975)
- **Confidence:** high

### A2. How the canal was blocked
- **Safe wording:** "Egypt sank ships to block the canal, and over the next years it filled with mines and wreckage."
- **Details:**
  - The Royal Navy clearance booklet says that in 1967 **nine Egyptian ships and a concrete caisson** were sunk to block the waterway. The ships were the *Mecca* (about 7,000 t, sunk 7 km south of Port Said), the *Ismailia*, the tanker *Magd* near Suez, three tugs and three dredgers. The same booklet says "twelve ships" on another page.
  - The US Nimrod Spar operation removed **10 designated wrecks** in 1974 (WP-CLEAR).
  - SCHARRNBECK says the military blocked the waterway with wrecks "a few days later". WP-SV gives 6 June 1967.
  - Mines and unexploded weapons built up over 1967–73. The RN booklet says huge amounts of ordnance were dropped or placed in the canal "during the seven years up to October 1973".
  - The 1974 clearance found:
    - about 7,500 unexploded items in the canal itself;
    - 686,000 land mines on the banks;
    - 118 pontoon-bridge sections, 102 boats and barges, trucks and tanks (WP-CLEAR).
  - The pontoon-bridge sections come from the **1973** army crossings, not 1967.
- **"Flooded the canal" is wrong.** Say "sank" or "scuttled".
- **Sources:** RN-1974 pp.3 and 27; WP-CLEAR; SCHARRNBECK; WP-EN; 99PI
- **Confidence:** high that ships were sunk. med for the exact number (9 plus a caisson, or 10, or 12). med for "mines" as a 1967 action. Several secondary sources say Egypt laid mines at both ends. The primary clearance sources describe ordnance that built up over 1967–73.

### A3. "They even sank a bridge"
- **Safe wording:** "They even blew up a railway bridge across the canal." Or simply leave the bridge out.
- **Details:**
  - The El Ferdan railway swing bridge (north of Ismailia) was **destroyed in 1967**. Egyptian engineering general Ahmed Hamdi ordered it blown up so that Israeli troops could not use it.
  - WP-EN says "ships, dredgers, other floating craft and even a bridge were sunk to block the canal". Its source is Popular Mechanics, May 1975, p.67, which I could not read.
  - The 1974 wreck-removal lists do **not** include a bridge. They list nine or ten ships plus a caisson. The bridge sections found in 1974 were 1973 pontoon bridges.
  - There is no evidence that a bridge was "dropped into the canal" as a blocking obstacle.
- **Sources:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ahmed_Hamdi ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/El_Ferdan_Railway_Bridge ; https://www.britannica.com/topic/Al-Firdan-Bridge ; WP-EN (citing Popular Mechanics 1975); Everything-Everywhere ("blew up a bridge"); RN-1974; WP-CLEAR
- **Confidence:** med that a bridge was blown up. **low** that a bridge was sunk to block the canal.

### A4. The lake became no-man's land
- **Safe wording:** "Israel's army on one shore, Egypt's on the other, and the ships stuck right in the middle."
- **Details:** After the war Israel held the whole east bank and Egypt held the west bank. HILL calls it a watery no-man's land. ZENITH says the crews could not go ashore.
- **Sources:** HILL; ZENITH; WP-EN; WP-PL
- **Confidence:** high

## B. Ships and countries

### B1. Count and location
- **Safe wording:** "Fifteen ships from eight countries got stuck. Fourteen of them sat together in the Great Bitter Lake."
- **Details:**
  - 14 ships were in the **Great Bitter Lake**. These 14 formed the Great Bitter Lake Association (GBLA); its badge carries the number 14.
  - A 15th ship, the US *Observer*, was cut off alone in **Lake Timsah** further north, near Ismailia. It was not a GBLA member. HAPAG-1975 says its crew nicknamed it the "F.B.I.", the Forgotten Boat of Ismailia.
  - Wikipedia lists *Observer* as a tanker carrying wheat from Galveston to Bombay. TIME-1969 calls it a freighter.
- **Sources:** HILL (title page: 14 ships of eight nations); TIME-1969; SEEMOTIVE; WP-EN; WP-DE
- **Confidence:** high

- **Confidence:** high

### B3. Did any ship sink during the 8 years?
- **Safe wording:** "Only one never left. In 1973, the American ship African Glen was hit and sank."
- **Details:**
  - *African Glen* was hit during the Yom Kippur War, apparently on **16 Oct 1973** (shipstamps.co.uk), and settled on the bottom of the shallow lake. Its masts and funnel stayed above water. It was refloated in 1977 (shipstamps.co.uk; I could not open that page directly).
  - What hit it is unclear:
    - "Israeli jets", because Egyptian soldiers were using it as an observation post (WP-DE, IMarEST);
    - an "Israeli rocket" or a "stray missile" (Everything-Everywhere, 99PI, WP-SV);
    - Israeli shells and rockets (shipstamps).
  - 99PI says no one was killed. By 1972 only about two caretakers lived aboard (HILL/Fischer).
- **Sources:** WP-EN; WP-DE; WP-SV; 99PI; IMarEST; https://shipstamps.co.uk/forum/viewtopic.php?p=7565
- **Confidence:** high that it sank in 1973. low on who or what hit it, and why.

## C. Life on the lake

### C1. Duration
- **Safe wording:** "The ships were stuck for almost eight years." ("Eight years" is acceptable rounding.)
- **Details:**
  - Trapped from 5 June 1967.
  - The ships left the lake in **May 1975**: WP-DE, WP-SV (*Killara* and *Nippon* towed to Piraeus in May 1975), HAPAG-1975 (the remaining crew flew out on 2 May 1975).
  - The canal reopened officially on **5 June 1975**, eight years to the day after the war began.
  - The German ships reached Hamburg on 24 May 1975, before the official reopening.
  - Time in the lake was about 7 years and 11 months (5 Jun 1967 to 24 May 1975 is about 2,910 days).
- **Sources:** WP-DE; WP-SV; HAPAG-1975; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Closure_of_the_Suez_Canal_(1967%E2%80%931975)
- **Confidence:** high

### C2. Why "Yellow Fleet"
- **Safe wording:** "Desert sandstorms kept coating the ships in yellow dust, so people called them the Yellow Fleet."
- **Details:**
  - Katzler (ZENITH): because of the desert storms, everything on the ships kept turning yellow from the sand.
  - SCHARRNBECK: after every sandstorm the ships had a yellow film on them. The crews even wrote a GBLA anthem to the tune of the Beatles' "Yellow Submarine".
  - IMarEST: from a distance the fleet looked yellowish.
  - The ships were **not buried in sand and not painted yellow**. It was a dusty yellow film or tint.
- **Sources:** ZENITH; SCHARRNBECK; 99PI; WP-EN; WP-DE; IMarEST; SEEMOTIVE
- **Confidence:** high for the sand-dust explanation. med that the name was used in the crews' own time; it is mostly attested later.

### C3. Crews: the critical trap
- **Safe wording:** "The ships were stuck for eight years, but the sailors weren't. Crews took turns, usually staying a few months before fresh crews flew in."
- **Details:**
  - **The original crews and passengers went home in summer and autumn 1967.** On *Münsterland*, 22 people left on 25 June 1967, and the rest of the original crew was relieved in August 1967 (HAPAG-1975; MARE). British relief crews left London on 23 Aug 1967 (HILL photo caption).
  - **Rotation periods differ by source:**
    - every 3 months: TIME-1969 (contemporary), WP-EN, WP-SV;
    - 3 to 4 months: AmusingPlanet;
    - 3 to 9 months: Fischer, 1972, in HILL;
    - every 6 months: MARE, WP-DE.
  - **Real tours:** Scharrnbeck 7 months (Oct 1967 to 5 May 1968); Hill about 4 months (Apr to Aug 1968); Katzler about 6 months (Jun to Dec 1969).
  - **Skeleton crews:**
    - *Münsterland* went from 44 crew plus 10 passengers in June 1967 to 21 by Oct 1967, then 13, then 10.
    - From 1969 the ships were **tied together** side by side in groups, using ropes with fenders in between, each group run by about 10 men.
    - The groups were *Müwinikies* (*Münsterland, Nordwind, Nippon, Killara, Essayons*), *Ledmelaga* (*Lednice, Melampus, Agapenor*) and *Djakbier* (later *Djabiporst*, with the Polish ships, *Port Invercargill* and *Scottish Star*). *Vasil Levski* and *African Glen* stayed single.
    - In July 1972 the German crews were replaced by four men from a Norwegian firm.
  - **About 3,000 sailors** served over the eight years. 99PI adds that one woman served too.
  - Total headcount at any one time: about 200 in 1969 (Katzler, ZENITH), but only about 50 by 1970 (HAPAG-1975). Avoid giving a number.
- **Sources:** HAPAG-1975; MARE; SCHARRNBECK; ZENITH; TIME-1969; HILL (Fischer p.183); SEEMOTIVE; 99PI; Master Mariners (Senker)
- **Confidence:** high that crews rotated and nobody stayed the whole eight years. med on the exact rotation length.

### C4. Egyptian restrictions
- **Safe wording:** "They couldn't go ashore. Radio was banned, and every letter was read."
- **Details:**
  - No going ashore: the lake was no-man's land (ZENITH). An Egyptian soldier sat on guard at the gangway (ZENITH).
  - Radio traffic was generally forbidden; telegrams went through the agent in Cairo; Egyptian censors read every letter (SCHARRNBECK).
  - Ships were not allowed to move after dark (ZENITH). Scharrnbeck did get permission to run his ship in circles once a month to keep the engine working.
  - Relief crews came in by bus from Cairo and a launch, watched by military police (SCHARRNBECK; ZENITH).
- **Sources:** ZENITH; SCHARRNBECK
- **Confidence:** high (two first-hand captains)

### C5. Great Bitter Lake Association (GBLA)
- **Safe wording:** "So they started their own club, the Great Bitter Lake Association."
- **Details:**
  - Declared on **Saturday 7 October 1967** aboard the British ship *Melampus*. HILL says the idea came up at an earlier party, and MARE credits Capt. Jim Starkey.
  - Purpose: friendship and mutual help across all 14 ships.
  - Rituals and symbols:
    - a GBLA buoy in the middle of the lake, topped with an anchor and the number "14";
    - GBLA ties, blazer and car badges and beer tankards;
    - a special "Water Baby" tie for anyone who fell into the lake (HILL);
    - a captain's pennant for any master who had commanded on the lake;
    - a GBLA flag;
    - an anthem to the tune of "Yellow Submarine" (SCHARRNBECK);
    - HILL says the GBLA constitution called it an "International Federal Republic".
  - The association disbanded in 1975 (MentalFloss). Reunions continued; about 100 people met in Liverpool on 1 June 2017, and BBC One Show filmed it (Master Mariners/FreightWaves).
- **Sources:** HILL pp.8–9; HILL (Fischer); MARE; SEEMOTIVE; WP-EN; 99PI
- **Confidence:** high for the date and the ship. med for the individual rituals; most come from HILL alone.

### C6. "Bitter Lake Olympics" 1968
- **Safe wording:** "In 1968, the year of the Mexico City Olympics, they held their own Bitter Lake Olympics. Poland won."
- **Details:**
  - **Dates:** 26 Sep to 12 Oct 1968 (XYZ) or 28 Sep to 12 Oct 1968 (HAPAG-1975). HILL and SEEMOTIVE say October 1968; MARE says summer 1968. The Mexico City Games ran **12–27 Oct 1968**, so the lake games mostly happened **just before** Mexico and ended as Mexico opened. "At the same time as Mexico" is not exact.
  - HAPAG-1975 and MentalFloss mistakenly write "Tokyo Olympics".
  - **Organisers:** the Polish ships (HILL; MARE says *Djakarta*; HAPAG-1975 says on the Poles' suggestion).
  - **Scale:** **14 events** (AmusingPlanet, MentalFloss, SEEMOTIVE, MARE, Vice, Master Mariners). Crews from eight nations took part.
  - **Events named in two or more sources:** sailing and rowing in lifeboats, swimming, diving, high jump, sprinting, archery, shooting (air rifle), weightlifting, water polo, football, table tennis. Single mentions: fishing, acrobatics, long jump, triathlon.
  - **Results:** 1st **Poland**, 2nd West Germany, 3rd Britain (AmusingPlanet; Everything-Everywhere; XYZ). XYZ gives medal counts: Poland 4 gold, 4 silver, 5 bronze; Germany 3 gold, 5 silver, 4 bronze.
  - **Medals:** HILL (insider) says special medallions were made in Poland and Germany. SEEMOTIVE and MentalFloss say "handmade" or "hand-crafted"; Vice says the Polish crews made them. Say "real medals" or just "medals".
- **Sources:** HILL p.8; HAPAG-1975; MARE; XYZ; AmusingPlanet; Everything-Everywhere; SEEMOTIVE; MentalFloss; Vice; ZENITH
- **Confidence:** high that the games took place in 1968 with 14 events. med-high that Poland won. med on the medal detail.

### C7. Football on deck
- **Safe wording:** "They played football on the deck of a British ship, with nets around it to keep the ball from flying into the lake."
- **Details:**
  - The ship was *Port Invercargill*: HILL, TIME-1969, MARE, XYZ, AmusingPlanet. ZENITH and XYZ describe nets around the deck and four-a-side games.
  - From June 1968 there was a "Suez Little World Cup" with a silver cup donated by the Daily Express (HILL). There were monthly matches; Fischer's account mentions a "prize-giving party".
  - Do **not** call it "the biggest ship" (TIME-1969 and AmusingPlanet do). *Killara* (about 11,000–13,000 GRT) and *Observer* (17,614 GRT) were larger by tonnage.
- **Confidence:** high

### C8. Cinema, pool, church, doctor, parties
- **Cinema:**
  - **Safe wording:** "Movie nights, with the Bulgarian ship as the cinema."
  - Films were shown on *Vasil Levski* (TIME-1969; WP-PL; XYZ says it was open-air). The shipping companies sent films and crews swapped reels (SCHARRNBECK; 99PI).
  - Confidence: high
- **Pool:**
  - **Safe wording:** "One Swedish ship even had a pool."
  - The pool was on *Killara*. WP-EN cites National Geographic, June 1975; also AmusingPlanet, WP-PL and XYZ. 99PI says there was a gym on "the Swedish ship".
  - Confidence: med-high
- **"Church":**
  - **Safe wording:** "Every Sunday they met on the German ship Nordwind, and jokingly called it church."
  - Officers came by lifeboat, the bells of five ships were rung together, and letters were stamped (HILL/Fischer; SCHARRNBECK; HAPAG-1975). HILL says the English coined the name "church".
  - It was mostly a social gathering. **Not for kids:** it was a Sunday-morning drink.
  - Confidence: high
- **Doctor:**
  - **Safe wording:** "A Polish ship had a doctor, so it became everyone's hospital."
  - HILL and XYZ say *Djakarta*. SCHARRNBECK says *Bolesław Bierut*, with a daily surgery from 8 to 11. 99PI and Senker just say "the Polish ship(s)".
  - Confidence: high for "a Polish ship". The specific ship is disputed.
- **Other activities:**
  - lifeboats rigged as sailing boats, weekly regattas and a yacht club;
  - water-skiing behind *Lednice*'s boat;
  - table tennis, darts, card and board games, fishing;
  - barbecues and fancy-dress parties.
  - Sources: HILL; HAPAG-1975; ZENITH; SCHARRNBECK; Master Mariners.
  - Confidence: high

### C9. Trading food between ships
- **Safe wording:** "They swapped food between ships: German eggs for Swedish fish, French shrimp for British apples."
- **Details:**
  - The captains pooled their cargo lists (HILL p.7):
    - *Münsterland*: frozen lamb, beef, butter and fresh eggs from Australia;
    - *Nippon*: frozen fish from Japan;
    - Blue Funnel ships: tea, canned chicken and canned oranges;
    - *Sindh*: frozen prawns and shrimp;
    - *Port Invercargill* and *Scottish Star*: Australian apples and pears, and wine;
    - *Killara* could make fresh milk.
  - HAPAG-1975 says a Swedish ship was nicknamed the "**Bitter Lake Cow**" because it made milk from egg and milk powder.
  - Owners agreed the swaps by cable, with receipts (HILL). The crews also fished and caught lobsters (MARE).
- **Sources:** HILL; HAPAG-1975; MARE; SCHARRNBECK; ZENITH; 99PI
- **Confidence:** high

### C10. Friendship across the Iron Curtain
- **Safe wording:** "Sailors from East and West, whose countries were rivals, became real friends."
- **Details:**
  - SCHARRNBECK: deep friendships grew, and in the middle of a war the crews became a symbol of international understanding.
  - HILL: a great spirit of friendship.
  - Nuance (ZENITH): Polish crews still had to attend political evenings, and a Polish doctor used to slip away to the German ship.
- **Confidence:** high

## D. Stamps

### D1. Homemade stamps
- **Safe wording:** "They even made their own stamps, and some letters carrying them really got delivered."
- **Details:**
  - **First stamps:** made autumn to December 1967 by the captain of the Czechoslovak *Lednice*. HILL/Fischer names him "George Kudrna" and XYZ "Jiří Kudrna", but Czech philatelic sources name the Lednice captains as Benda and Šlechta. SCHARRNBECK also says a Czech captain of *Lednice* had the idea.
  - The Poles made the best-looking ones: lobsters, crabs, a moon-landing motif (ZENITH; XYZ).
  - **How they were made** (HILL/Fischer, 1972):
    - linocut, carbon or hand-cast printing blocks;
    - "butter yellow" ink from the ship's baker;
    - one sailor stood barefoot on the printing block to press it;
    - perforated with a **sewing machine and an injection needle**.
    - 99PI adds crayons, potato skins and coffee grounds.
  - **Volume:** HILL catalogued **190 issues and 784 different stamps** from 1967 to 1974. Fischer estimated about 3,000 letters by 1971, of which about 750 survive.
  - **Ship postmarks:** each ship had a "Mailed on Board" cachet with an anchor, "14" and "GBLA". From 1969 there were group names on the stamps (*Müwinikies*, *Djakbier*, *Ledmelaga*...). A stamp office was set up on *Nordwind*.
- **Sources:** HILL (Fischer pp.181–185); SEEMOTIVE; SCHARRNBECK; ZENITH; 99PI; XYZ
- **Confidence:** high

### D2. Were they recognised by the Egyptian post? (contested; do not overstate)
- **Safe wording:** "Most letters still needed real Egyptian stamps, but a few got home with only the sailors' homemade ones."
- **For recognition:**
  - TIME-1969 says Egyptian postal authorities "graciously allow" the stamps as legal postage.
  - WP-EN (citing TIME), WP-DE, WP-PL, IMarEST and Vice repeat this.
  - SCHARRNBECK says the letters were postmarked by the Egyptian post.
  - 99PI says "some were accepted".
- **Against recognition (philatelic and insider sources):**
  - Fischer & Müller (1972, in HILL): the stamps had **no franking value**. Mail went through Suez or Cairo post offices with **Egyptian stamps added**; only "a small number" of letters with only GBLA stamps reached their destination.
  - The **Swedish Post Office warned** Swedish sailors that sending letters with only GBLA stamps was illegal (HILL).
  - HILL himself argued the stamps "should eventually be properly recognized", which means they were not yet.
  - SEEMOTIVE: "keine gültige Frankaturkraft" (no valid postage value).
  - Czech philatelic magazine Infofila: the stamps were not recognised as postage, and Egyptian stamps were required.
- **Conclusion:** they were **not** official Egyptian postage. Egypt's post tolerated and postmarked letters that carried them, usually alongside Egyptian stamps, and some covers got through without Egyptian stamps (the Corinphila lot description confirms this).
- **Sources:** TIME-1969; HILL; SEEMOTIVE; http://www.infofila.cz/nepokojny-egypt-r-2-c-3876 ; https://corinphila.ch/en/_auctions/&action=showLot&auctionID=7&lotno=571 ; 99PI
- **Confidence:** high for the safe wording. **low** for "recognised by the Egyptian post".

### D3. Collector value today
- **Safe wording:** "Today collectors hunt for them."
- **Details:**
  - The Corinphila (Switzerland) lot was the late Peter Bottrill's GBLA collection, with 240+ covers and described as "probably the most complete" in existence. It started at CHF 1,000 and sold for **CHF 3,400**.
  - David Feldman offered a small GBLA lot in June 2026; I could not read the price.
  - There is no reliable price per stamp. SEEMOTIVE warns of Italian-made imitations.
- **Confidence:** high that they are collectors' items. med for the auction figure. Avoid any price per stamp.

## E. The return

### E1. Who sailed home under their own power
- **Safe wording:** "When the canal finally opened, only the two German ships could sail home on their own engines."
- **Details:**
  - *Münsterland* and *Nordwind* were the only ships that left under their own power: WP-DE, SEEMOTIVE, IMarEST, WP-EN ("reportedly"), and SCHARRNBECK (the only ships to reach their home ports under their own power).
  - In March 1975 the German captain told Visnews that the others would probably have to be towed.
  - *Münsterland* had been kept in good condition and got a provisional seaworthiness certificate (HAPAG-1975).
- **Sources:** as listed; https://reuters.screenocean.com/record/1066152 (description seen in search results only)
- **Confidence:** high

### E2. Arrival in Hamburg, 24 May 1975
- **Safe wording:** "On May 24th, 1975, they reached Hamburg, and tens of thousands of people cheered them home."
- **Details:**
  - Arrival around noon (MARE). Fireboats sprayed water fountains, tug sirens sounded, and yachts escorted the ships (MARE; SCHARRNBECK).
  - **Crowd size is contested:**
    - "more than 30,000": WP-EN (citing an NDR page that is now dead), SEEMOTIVE, XYZ, Everything-Everywhere, AmusingPlanet;
    - "**nearly 100,000** people lined the banks": MARE 2003;
    - "**tens of thousands**" cheered at the Landungsbrücken: SCHARRNBECK.
  - "Tens of thousands" covers all of these.
- **Confidence:** high for the date and arrival. med for "30,000" as an exact figure. Use "tens of thousands" or "huge crowds".

### E3. "8 years, 3 months, 5 days"
- **Safe wording:** "Münsterland's trip to Australia and back took eight years, three months and five days."
- **Details:**
  - The figure appears in SCHARRNBECK, SEEMOTIVE and WP-EN; WP-EN calls it "the end of a voyage to Australia".
  - **Counted back from 24 May 1975 it starts on about 19 Feb 1967 = 3,016 days.** MARE writes "exactly 3,016 days", but wrongly calls that the time in the lake. So the number covers the **whole round trip from Hamburg**, which began before the war, not the time trapped.
  - The time in the lake was about 7 years and 11 months.
  - WP-SV mistakenly says "from Australia".
  - *Münsterland* was on its 18th voyage from Australia to Europe (HAPAG-1975).
  - **Cargo** (HAPAG-1975; WP-DE): about 3,000 t of steel and steel wire, about 2,000 t of wool, over 1,000 t of special sand, lead, hides, canned fruit, jam, agricultural machinery, **five tons of kitchen clocks**; in the cold stores, meat, butter, grapes, 300 t of pears and **millions of eggs**.
  - **Egg count is disputed:** about 7.5 million (SCHARRNBECK); "almost 8 million" (MARE; HAPAG-1975). HAPAG-1975 also gives "57 tons" of eggs, which cannot be 8 million eggs (that would weigh about 450 t). Say "millions of eggs".
  - By 1975 the canned food was ruined, but the wool, hides, sand, lead and steel wire had largely survived in the dry air (HAPAG-1975).
- **Confidence:** high for the figure itself. med for the interpretation, which is my arithmetic plus MARE's 3,016 days.

### E4. What happened to the other 13
- **Safe wording:** "Most of the others had to be towed away, and many ended up as scrap."
- **Details** (WP-DE table unless noted):
  - *Killara* and *Nippon*: towed to Piraeus in May 1975 (WP-SV). *Killara* went to Hellenic Lines and was scrapped in 1986 (kommandobryggan.se). *Nippon* became *Marit*, then *Hellenic Patriot*.
  - *Djakarta* and *Bolesław Bierut*: taken to Greece and sold (WP-PL). *Djakarta*, as *Manina III*, ran aground in 1981.
  - The **British wrote off all four ships as insurance losses** (AmusingPlanet; Everything-Everywhere). *Agapenor* became *Nikos* (scrapped 1981); *Melampus* became *Annoula II* (scrapped 1983); *Scottish Star* was laid up in Piraeus 1975–79, then scrapped; *Port Invercargill* became *Kavo Kolne* (scrapped 1979).
  - *Vasil Levski*: scrapped at Split in 1975.
  - *Lednice*: returned to service. WP-DE (citing taz 1994) says it later hit a mine off Sri Lanka in 1994; low confidence.
  - *African Glen*: sank in 1973 and was refloated in 1977.
  - *Essayons* (ex-*Sindh*) and *Observer*: fate not documented.
  - *Münsterland* went back into service and was scrapped in 1984. *Nordwind* was scrapped in 1985.
- **Confidence:** high that most were towed or written off. med on individual fates.

## F. Extras: the 1973 war, Ever Given and other details

### F1. Fighting around the ships (1969–70 and 1973)
- **Safe wording:** "Fighter jets roared over their masts, and in 1973 the war came right to the edge of their lake."
- **Details:**
  - **War of Attrition, 1969–70:** dogfights between Israeli Mirages and Egyptian MiGs overhead; tanks firing across the lake; shell casings landing on deck; crews sheltering below deck (Katzler, ZENITH).
  - A jet's drop tank, still half full of fuel, fell into the lake about 100 yards from *Nippon* (HAPAG-1975).
  - Fighter jets flew low over the masts in 1967–68 (SCHARRNBECK).
  - **No crew member was harmed** in eight years, per HAPAG-1975. 99PI says no one was killed when *African Glen* sank.
  - **1973 (Yom Kippur War):** from 15–16 Oct 1973 Israeli forces crossed the canal at **Deversoir, at the northern tip of the Great Bitter Lake** (Operation Abirey-Halev; Battle of the Chinese Farm, "north of the Great Bitter Lake"). IMarEST says the ships were "caught in the crossfire".
  - Israel then built a **causeway across the canal at Deversoir**, a four-lane road, which closed off the lake to the north even more. The first ship passed through the breached causeway on 25 June 1974 (RN-1974).
  - The only ship damage recorded in 1973 is the sinking of *African Glen* (B3).
- **Sources:** ZENITH; HAPAG-1975; SCHARRNBECK; IMarEST; RN-1974; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Battle_of_the_Chinese_Farm ; https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Operation_Abirey-Halev
- **Confidence:** high for the 1969–70 shelling and dogfights, and for the 1973 crossing at the lake's north end. low for any other ship damage in 1973; none is documented.

### F2. Ever Given 2021
- **Safe wording:** "In 2021, the Ever Given blocked the canal for six days. These ships were stuck for almost eight years."
- **Details:** Blocked 23 to 29 March 2021, about 151 hours from grounding on the morning of 23 March to refloating on the afternoon of 29 March. "Six days" is correct.
- **Source:** https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/2021_Suez_Canal_obstruction
- **Confidence:** high

## H. Open conflicts between sources

1. **Convoy direction.** The convoy was northbound (HILL, HAPAG, SCHARRNBECK, MARE, the Wikipedias). AmusingPlanet says southward; the NYT 1974 URL slug says "headed south" (article not read). There is also disagreement about the opposite convoy: HILL says the southbound convoy was cancelled at Port Said, HAPAG-1975 says it cleared the canal, and SCHARRNBECK says it passed them in the lake.
2. **Number of blockships.** The RN booklet says "nine Egyptian ships and a concrete caisson" on one page and "twelve ships" on another. The US Nimrod Spar operation removed ten designated wrecks.
3. **The bridge.** WP-EN (citing Popular Mechanics 1975) says a bridge was sunk. The 1974 clearance records list no 1967 bridge. Other sources say the El Ferdan railway bridge was "destroyed" or "blown up".
4. **Rotation length.** 3 months (TIME-1969, WP-EN, WP-SV); 3–4 months (AmusingPlanet); 3–9 months (Fischer); 6 months (MARE, WP-DE).
5. **People on the lake in 1969–70.** About 200 (Katzler, ZENITH) versus about 50 by 1970 (HAPAG-1975).
6. **Stamp recognition.** For: TIME-1969 and the Wikipedias. Against: Fischer, HILL, SEEMOTIVE, Infofila and the Swedish post warning. See D2.
7. **Which Polish ship had the doctor.** *Djakarta* (HILL, XYZ) or *Bolesław Bierut* (SCHARRNBECK).
8. **Who designed the first stamps.** The Lednice captain was "George/Jiří Kudrna" according to HILL and XYZ, but Czech sources name the Lednice captains as Benda and Šlechta.
9. **Medals.** Made in Poland and Germany (HILL) versus handmade (SEEMOTIVE, MentalFloss).
10. **Olympics dates.** 26 Sep or 28 Sep to 12 Oct 1968 (XYZ, HAPAG); "October 1968" (HILL); "summer 1968" (MARE).
11. **Hamburg crowd.** 30,000+ (WP-EN, SEEMOTIVE) versus nearly 100,000 (MARE) versus "tens of thousands" (SCHARRNBECK).
12. **Egg count.** About 7.5 million (SCHARRNBECK) versus almost 8 million (MARE, HAPAG). HAPAG's "57 t" is inconsistent with either.
13. **African Glen.** Hit by jets because it was used as an observation post (WP-DE, IMarEST) versus a stray rocket or missile (99PI, WP-SV) versus shells and rockets on 16 Oct 1973 (shipstamps).
14. **Time stuck.** "8 years" (common) versus "almost 8 years" (WP-DE, HAPAG). "3016 days" (MARE) is actually Münsterland's whole voyage.
15. **Number of nationalities.** MARE and WP-SV say "seven", but list or imply eight. HILL says eight.
16. **Size of the football ship.** TIME-1969 and AmusingPlanet call *Port Invercargill* the largest ship, which is contradicted by the tonnage tables.
17. **Cargo in 1975.** HAPAG-1975 says Münsterland's wool and steel survived on board. Fischer (1972) says the ships had been "completely unloaded" of perishables, many dumped in the lake. It is unclear whether the cargo was ever delivered.
